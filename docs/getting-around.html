Buses, bikes and boots

Getting around Castlegregory and the Dingle Peninsula

There is a bus, and it is better than the reputation of rural Irish transport would suggest. Local Link route 273 links Tralee with Castlegregory, Cloghane and Brandon, five times a day and later on Friday and Saturday nights.

Using the 273

Coming out for the evening, the useful westbound is the one that leaves Tralee bus station at 17:32, Denny Street 17:37 and Prince's Street 17:40, reaching Maunsell's in Castlegregory at 18:10. The next westbound after that does not arrive until 20:21.

Getting home again is the harder half. The last eastbound of the day leaves Castlegregory at 20:30 on Fridays and Saturdays, reaching Prince's Street at 21:00 and the bus station at 21:08. Midweek the last one is earlier still, at 18:30, and there is no late service on a Sunday. Only two of the five daily journeys run beyond Castlegregory to Cloghane and Brandon, and the last one back from Brandon leaves at 16:00, so an evening out there needs a car, a lift or a taxi.

Full timetable, fares and stops are on the TFI route 273 leaflet. No booking is needed: turn up and pay the driver, in cash or with a Leap card. Local Link Kerry are on 066 714 7002 and do sometimes lay on extra journeys for local events, so it is worth a call.

The bus

TFI Local Link Kerry route 273, Cé Bhréanainn to Tralee, is the one that matters. It runs Brandon, Cloghane, Stradbally, Castlegregory, Aughacasla, Camp, Curraheen and Blennerville into Tralee, calling at Prince's Street, Denny Street and the bus station, and continuing to University Hospital Kerry and the MTU campus. It launched in June 2024 under Connecting Ireland.

Frequency is the thing to understand. There are five return journeys a day between Castlegregory and Tralee, Monday to Saturday, and four on Sunday. Only two of those extend beyond Castlegregory to Cloghane and Brandon. There is a late service on Friday and Saturday nights between Castlegregory and Tralee. Check the timetable before you rely on it.

Bus Éireann route 275, Tralee to Dingle, runs along the south side of the peninsula through Camp, Annascaul and Lispole, about ten journeys each way on weekdays and far fewer at weekends. A new timetable took effect on 23 August 2026.

There is no bus over the Conor Pass. To reach Dingle town by public transport from this coast you change at Camp from the 273 to the 275, or go all the way into Tralee and back out. We could not confirm that the two timetables are designed to connect, so leave yourself room.

The Maharees and Fahamore have no year-round bus. A pilot summer shuttle ran on four peak weekends in 2026, linking Castlegregory with Fahamore, Kilshannig and the Béal Geal beach car park, timed to meet the 273 and asking a two euro donation. Whether it returns is not yet announced.

The train

Tralee Casement is the nearest station and the most westerly in Europe, with roughly seven or eight trains a day on weekdays and a reduced Sunday service. Dublin is about four hours, Cork about two and a half, and there are connections from Limerick and Galway. From the station, take the 273 or a taxi.

There used to be a closer option. The Tralee and Dingle Light Railway ran a branch down into Castlegregory from 1891 until 1939. It is on the history page.

Taxis

Worth having a number in your phone before the evening rather than after it, particularly once the last bus has gone. Most of these are one or two cars, so ring ahead rather than expecting to hail one.

On a bike

The Wild Atlantic Way's Dingle Peninsula stage runs from Tralee to Castlemaine and takes in both this coast and the Conor Pass. The pass is one of the hardest climbs in the country: about seven kilometres at five and a half per cent from the Dingle side, with a maximum of eleven, and a single-lane road where cars have to reverse to pass each other. Ride it early in the day.

Castlegregory publishes eight local routes, among them a Camp and Maharees loop, a family cycle around the village, a run out to Glanteenassig Forest Park and a Conor Pass circuit. These are suggested routes rather than waymarked ones. There is no traffic-free greenway on this coast; the nearest is the Tralee to Fenit greenway on the old railway line, on the far side of Tralee Bay.

For hire, The Beach Box in Castlegregory rents bikes for fifteen euro a day and hands out route maps. Tralee Bike Rental and Dingle Bikes are the other options, both with delivery.

On foot

The Dingle Way circles the peninsula, and three of its eight stages are on this coast: Feothanach to Cloghane, Cloghane to Castlegregory, and Castlegregory back to Tralee. The middle one is the great one, twenty-nine kilometres and seven to nine hours, graded moderate: a few kilometres of quiet road, then eleven kilometres of Fermoyle Strand, then dune and field edge and more beach into Castlegregory. Watch the tide. After some high springs you may need to climb onto the dune around the six-kilometre mark and come back down when it is safe. Dogs are not permitted on the Way.

Cosán na Naomh, the Saints' Road, runs 17.7 kilometres from Ventry Strand to Ballybrack at the foot of Mount Brandon, waymarked, moderate, about five and a half hours, and it is one of Ireland's designated pilgrim paths.

Mount Brandon from Faha above Cloghane is 14.5 kilometres and about four hours, with 953 metres of ascent, graded strenuous, up a glaciated valley of paternoster lakes with a short scramble at the back of it to reach the col. The Saints' Road from Baile Breac is the gentler way up. Más an Tiompáin from Teer is 12.5 kilometres. All of these need an Ordnance Survey map, proper boots and a look at the forecast; the summit is rarely clear.

Accessibility

Around ninety per cent of Local Link services are wheelchair accessible and TFI asks you to ring ahead to book a space. Bus Éireann is fully accessible and does not require advance booking. Irish Rail asks twenty-four hours' notice for boarding assistance. We could not find published accessibility information for individual stops, beaches or trailheads on this coast.
