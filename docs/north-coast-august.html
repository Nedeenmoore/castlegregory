What to expect

The north coast in August

August on this coast is long-evening country. On the first of the month the sun sets at half past nine and there is usable light until after ten; by the thirty-first it sets at half past eight. This far west the sun is due south at about a quarter to two in the afternoon, which is why the evenings run so late, and why a concert at half past seven still leaves you daylight to get home by.

The weather is mild and unreliable in roughly equal measure. Met Éireann's nearest long-record station is Valentia Observatory, across Dingle Bay, where August averages a daily maximum of 18 degrees and a minimum of just under 13. August is not the sunniest month here; May is. Summer brings the lightest winds of the year, around eight knots at Valentia, which is welcome in a village hall and less welcome if you came to windsurf. Mount Brandon stands directly behind this coast, and the mountainous south-west is the wettest part of Ireland, so bring a coat you can walk in and do not plan the day around the forecast.

The sea is about as warm as it gets. Fifteen and a half degrees is the August average off Dingle, which is swimmable for as long as you are willing, and there is a wood-fired sauna at either end of the Maharees for afterwards.

What you will actually see

The dunes are in flower. Wild thyme, kidney vetch, sand pansy, hop trefoil, squinancywort, yellow rattle and strawberry clover are all out in August, along with sea holly, thrift and the harebells. The hedges along the lanes carry fuchsia and montbretia, both introductions and both unmistakably west of Ireland in high summer, and the blackberries start to come in over the last fortnight of the month.

August is the beginning of the autumn seabird passage, and Brandon Point is the place for it. BirdWatch Ireland gives August to early November for seawatching there, in onshore north-westerlies, when large movements of Manx and sooty shearwaters and skuas pass the head. Bottlenose dolphins are in Brandon Bay: the Irish Whale and Dolphin Group encountered them on every trip of its Tralee and Brandon Bays survey, including a large group in Brandon Bay on the sixth of August, and matched them to the Shannon Estuary population.

In the dune slacks around Lough Gill there are natterjack toadlets, thumbnail-sized, the year's young from Ireland's only toad. Castlegregory holds the largest breeding population in the country, and the National Parks and Wildlife Service releases head-started toadlets here each summer. The famous rasping chorus is a spring event, so do not expect to hear them, but look where you put your feet.

What else is on

The Brandon Regatta, a series of currach races with crews coming from along the west coast, is held on one of the last Sundays in August, with music and song afterwards. In Cloghane, Tinteán Ceoil, the replica cottage kitchen, runs traditional sessions on Monday evenings through the summer.

Two of the area's best-known events are in July, not August, and are often misplaced: Féile Lúghnasa at Cloghane and Brandon falls on the last weekend of July, as does Domhnach Chrom Dubh, the Mount Brandon pilgrimage; the Castlegregory Summer Festival, running since 1948, is the second weekend of July.

Further afield the same fortnight is busy across Kerry. Puck Fair at Killorglin runs 10 to 12 August 2026, the Rose of Tralee 14 to 18 August with the Rose Tour the week before, and National Heritage Week 15 to 23 August. Tralee is forty minutes away. All of it competes for beds and road space, which is the practical reason to book early.

Crowds, and treading lightly

The Maharees is busy at peak season, busy enough that a pilot shuttle bus ran on the late July and early August weekends of 2026 expressly to reduce traffic. The Maharees Conservation Association, which has planted marram and built two kilometres of chestnut fencing to hold the dunes together, asks visitors to keep off the dunes, use the designated beach accesses, camp only in official sites and take litter home. The dune system has retreated by more than forty metres in recent decades. It is not a hardy landscape.
