On the water

Water sports on the Maharees

The Maharees is a tombolo, a five-kilometre spit of sand running north into the Atlantic with Brandon Bay on one side and Tralee Bay on the other. That geography is the whole story. One side or the other is nearly always sheltered, and because of the way the peninsula sits, the prevailing south-westerly is cross-shore somewhere along the bay whatever it is doing. Windsurfers have known this for decades. The Professional Windsurfers Association brought its Irish Wave Grand Prix here three times between 2000 and 2002, and Dingle Peninsula Tourism describes the area as legendary among windsurfers.

One honest note about August. The peak wind months here are the winter and shoulder ones, roughly October to April; summer wind is unreliable. Surf is a different matter, and the beach break works at any time of year. So come for the swimming, the surf, the flat water and the diving, and treat a windy August day as a bonus.

The beaches, and what each is for

The strand runs unbroken from Fermoyle round to the Maharees, one of the longest beaches in Ireland: sources give it as twelve miles from Fermoyle to Fahamore or fourteen kilometres from Cloghane, which do not agree, but either way it takes a day to walk.

Kilcummin is the gentle one, a beginner's wave, best at low to mid tide. Gowlane and Stradbally are typical beach breaks that often have waves when nowhere else does. Stoney Gap, between the two caravan parks, works in a north-westerly swell at low tide. Dumps is hollower than the rest and best at low to mid tide; at high tide it puts you on the stones. Mossies is a reef, left and right, mid to high tide, for the experienced only. Fermoyle sits in the lee of Brandon Head and the wind funnelling off the mountain there is gusty. Magherabeg, on the Tralee Bay side, is the flat-water spot and the sheltered one, and it is the Blue Flag beach.

Safety

Magherabeg is the only lifeguarded beach on this coast. The Irish bathing season runs from the start of June to mid-September, and Kerry's lifeguards are on duty roughly noon to seven on weekdays and eleven to seven at weekends, subject to staffing. The rule that matters: if there is no flag, there is no lifeguard.

None of the Brandon Bay surf beaches, Fermoyle, Stradbally, Kilcummin or Gowlane, is lifeguarded. The local warning for Stradbally is that currents there can be strong and dangerous. There is shorebreak at all the wave-facing beaches.

If you are caught in a rip, Water Safety Ireland's advice is to stay calm, not to fight it, and to swim out of it parallel to the shore; if you cannot, float or tread water, face the shore, wave and shout. Do not swim alone. Kerry County Council's shorthand is FLAG: find the flags, look at the signs, ask a lifeguard, get a friend to swim with you, and stick your hand up if you are in trouble.

Lough Gill

The shallow lagoon on the edge of the village is not a watersports lake. It averages thirty to forty centimetres deep, it is part of the Tralee Bay and Magharees Peninsula Special Area of Conservation, and it holds one of Ireland's most important natterjack toad populations along with wintering swans. Brown trout angling from a boat is the only water use we found documented. Walk the shore, bring binoculars, leave the water alone.

Prices, seasons and opening hours here are as published by the operators and change. Check before you travel, and book ahead in August.
